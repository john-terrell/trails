# Capability Repertoire Hierarchy

The structural half of [H-LAM/T](h-lam-t.md): the idea that everything a person can do is composed,
hierarchically, from a finite inventory of **basic capabilities** split between the human
and the artifacts. *"Each individual develops a certain repertoire of process capabilities
from which he selects and adapts those that will compose the processes that he executes"*
([Augmenting Human Intellect](Sources.md), §II.A).

## Definition

**Process hierarchy.** *"Every process of thought or action is made up of sub-processes."*
Making a pencil stroke from muscle movements; writing a character from strokes; writing
prose from planning, composing and dictating. He declines to look for the bottom: *"there
seems to be no way of telling whether or not the apparent 'bottoms'… exist in the physical
world or in the limitations of human understanding."*

**Three kinds of capability** (§II.A): *explicit-human* (inside the integument),
*explicit-artifact* (executed without human intervention), and *composite* (hierarchies
containing both). Any composite process decomposes eventually into the first two.

**Basic capabilities** are those that cannot be *usefully* changed (§II.C.5.a) — found by
recursive decomposition: take any capability, ask whether augmentation research could
usefully alter it, and keep descending until the answer is no. His worked example: a
particular finger motion is not basic (strength, speed and coordination are changeable),
but *"the basic movement range established by the mechanical-limit loci of the
muscle-tendon-bone system"* is.

**Two inventories.** Basic capabilities already in use can be found by analysing existing
hierarchies; those *not yet used* require a different search, of the kind technological
research performs. He also insists criteria must be fixed first — some research contexts
would disallow changes requiring extensive retraining, or *"undignified behavior by the
human."*

The tool-kit analogy carries the practical point: *"just as the mechanic must know what his
tools can do and how to use them, so the intellectual worker must know the capabilities of
his tools and have good methods, strategies, and rules of thumb for making use of them."*

## Why it matters

**It is why "little steps" is a design constraint rather than a lament.** *"The human mind
neither learns nor acts by large leaps, but by steps organized or structured so that each
one depends upon previous steps"* — so augmentation means exist to decompose large problems
until the steps suffice. Engelbart's §III.B refrain follows: *"the impressive new tricks all
are based upon lots of changes in the little things you do."*

**It explains why one innovation cascades.** *"A new process capability appearing anywhere
within that hierarchy can make practical a new consideration of latent change possibilities
in many other parts of the hierarchy."* His writing-machine example: one innovation
*"could trigger a rather extensive redesign of this hierarchy."* That is
[Knowledge Compounding](knowledge-compounding.md) at the level of capability rather than content.

**It gives a method for auditing a knowledge system.** Decompose what you actually do, find
the basic capabilities, and ask which are human and which are artifact. Applied to this
vault: reading and judgment are explicit-human; search, propagation, lint and export are
explicit-artifact; ingest is composite. The [Repetitive vs Creative Thought](repetitive-vs-creative-thought.md) line is
where the decomposition terminates.

The concept is real and load-bearing but is entirely dependent on [H-LAM/T](h-lam-t.md), where most
of its content already lives. It is paged separately because the *decomposition method* —
how to find basic capabilities — is a usable technique that would be buried inside a
larger page. Nothing beyond §II.A and §II.C.5.a supports it, and no source tests it.
